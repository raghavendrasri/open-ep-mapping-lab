Learning hub · Part 1 of 2 — Concepts

Seeing the heart's electricity in 3D

An electrophysiology (EP) mapping system turns the tiny electrical signals picked up by catheters inside the heart into a live, colour-coded 3D map — so a clinician can find exactly where an abnormal rhythm starts, how it travels, and where to treat it.

What it mapsActivation timing, voltage, and anatomy of a heart chamber
What it guidesCatheter navigation and ablation, often with little or no X-ray
What it treatsAF, atrial flutter, SVT, PVCs and ventricular tachycardia

The core idea

From heartbeat to map, in five links

Every mapping system — whatever the brand — is the same chain: sense the heart's local electrical signal, locate the electrode that sensed it in 3D space, and repeat that thousands of times until the points merge into a map. The differences between systems are in how they locate, how densely they sample, and how cleverly they interpret.

01 · SENSE

Electrogram

Electrodes on a catheter inside the chamber record the local activation signal — the electrogram (EGM) — at each spot they touch.

02 · LOCATE

3D position

A magnetic field, impedance field, or dielectric field tells the system where each electrode is, several times per second.

03 · BUILD

Geometry

Sweeping the catheter over the chamber wall traces its shape, building a 3D shell of the atrium or ventricle.

04 · COLOUR

Activation & voltage

Each point is coloured by when it fired (LAT) or how strong its signal was (voltage) — the map a clinician actually reads.

05 · TREAT

Ablation target

The map pinpoints the source or circuit. An ablation catheter delivers RF, cryo or pulsed-field energy there, and the map confirms the result.

What to look for

Find the earliest (red) point first — that is the source. Everything else is just the wave leaving it. Ablation targets the red spot itself, not the tissue it spreads into.

Key numbers

Cycle length ≈ 300 ms
A single source drives the whole chamber, so one well-placed lesion can end it.

Colour scale

Colours encode local activation time (LAT) — milliseconds relative to a reference electrogram, usually from a stable catheter in the coronary sinus.

The major mapping platforms, compared

Five platforms dominate modern EP labs. They all build the same kinds of maps — what differs is the localisation physics, the signature catheter, and the philosophy: closed and integrated, open to other brands' catheters, or built for maximum point density.

Reading the map

One chamber, many maps

The geometry stays the same; what changes is the value painted onto it. Choosing the right map type is most of the diagnostic skill.

Reading an electrogram

The map is only as good as the signals underneath it. These schematic traces show the four electrogram shapes a mapper learns to recognise on sight. Amplitude and timing — not the picture — are what the system measures.

Hardware

The catheters that do the sensing

High-density mapping became possible when catheters grew from 4 electrodes to dozens of small, closely spaced ones. Smaller electrodes and tighter spacing mean sharper, more local signals.

Multi-spline

PentaRay & OCTARAY (CARTO)

Flexible splines that splay against the chamber wall like a star — five splines carrying 20 electrodes on the PentaRay, eight splines carrying 48 on the OCTARAY — giving a wide footprint of points per placement.

Grid paddle

Advisor HD Grid (EnSite)

A flat 4 × 4 paddle of 16 electrodes. Because it samples along two axes at once, the system can compute omnipolar signals — the true maximum voltage regardless of the direction the wave crosses the paddle.

Mini-basket

IntellaMap Orion (OPAL HDx)

An expandable basket with 64 tiny electrodes on 8 splines. Paired with automated point acceptance, it collects very high-density maps quickly — the platform's defining strength.

Circular

Lasso-style circular catheters

A loop of electrodes that sits at a pulmonary vein opening and records the vein's potentials — the classic tool for confirming pulmonary vein isolation during AF ablation.

Ablation

Contact-force ablation catheters

The treatment catheter (e.g. ThermoCool SmartTouch, TactiFlex, IntellaNav StablePoint families) senses tip contact force in grams, so lesion tags on the map reflect pressure, power and time — not just position.

Non-contact

AcQMap balloon

A balloon carrying ultrasound transducers and electrodes that hovers in the chamber without touching the wall, reconstructing charge density over the whole chamber from a single beat.

Map-reading checklist

  • Reference stable? — LAT is only meaningful against a steady reference signal
  • Window set right? — the window of interest must cover the full cycle length
  • Early meets late? — in re-entry, red should sit next to purple around the circuit
  • Density sufficient? — gaps in points are guesses, not data
  • Voltage direction-checked? — a wave crossing electrodes end-on reads falsely low
  • Scar or artefact? — confirm low voltage with contact and a second look
  • EGMs inspected? — never trust the colour without reading the underlying signals
  • Endpoint proven? — isolation or block must be demonstrated, not assumed
